<TLDR>
A VAE uses an encoder to approximate the posterior over latent variables. It maximizes the ELBO, which balances reconstruction quality and a KL penalty.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

A Variational Autoencoder is a latent-variable model.

It assumes a latent variable $z$ and a decoder:

$$
p_\theta(x,z)=p(z)p_\theta(x \mid z).
$$

The prior is often a standard normal distribution.

$$
p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,I).
$$

The encoder approximates the true posterior.

$$
q_\phi(z \mid x) \approx p_\theta(z \mid x).
$$

The decoder maps a latent sample back to data space.

A VAE relates to <a href="/learn/general-ml/autoencoders">autoencoders</a>, but it models a distribution over latent codes.

## ELBO

The log marginal likelihood is:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
=
\log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz.
$$

This integral is often hard.

The Evidence Lower Bound is:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;x)
=
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}
[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)]
-
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z \mid x)\Vert p(z)).
$$

The first term rewards good reconstruction.

The KL term keeps the approximate posterior close to the prior.

Training usually minimizes the negative ELBO.

## Why the ELBO is a lower bound

The ELBO is less than or equal to $\log p_\theta(x)$.

It becomes tight when the encoder equals the true posterior.

<Proof title="ELBO from Jensen's inequality">
Start with the log marginal likelihood:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
=
\log \int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz.
$$

Multiply and divide by $q_\phi(z \mid x)$:

$$
=
\log \int q_\phi(z \mid x)
\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\,dz.
$$

Write the integral as an expectation:

$$
=
\log
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z \mid x)}
\right].
$$

Use Jensen's inequality:

$$
\ge
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}
\left[
\log
\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z \mid x)}
\right].
$$

Expand the joint distribution:

$$
=
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}
[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)]
-
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z \mid x)\Vert p(z)).
$$
</Proof>

## Reparameterization trick

The encoder often outputs a mean and log variance.

$$
q_\phi(z \mid x)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x),\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\phi^2(x))).
$$

Sample with:

$$
z = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x)\odot \epsilon,
\quad
\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I).
$$

This moves randomness outside the network.

Gradients can pass through $\mu_\phi$ and $\sigma_\phi$.

## Gaussian KL term

For a diagonal Gaussian encoder and a standard normal prior:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z \mid x)\Vert p(z))
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_i
\left(
\mu_i^2 + \sigma_i^2 - \log \sigma_i^2 - 1
\right).
$$

This term discourages latent codes from drifting far from the prior.

It also makes sampling possible by drawing $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$.

## Code example

```python
import torch

def reparameterize(mu, logvar):
    eps = torch.randn_like(mu)
    std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)
    return mu + std * eps

def gaussian_kl(mu, logvar):
    return 0.5 * torch.sum(
        mu.pow(2) + logvar.exp() - logvar - 1,
        dim=-1,
    )
```

## Common mistakes

Do not confuse the encoder with the true posterior.

Do not remove the KL term if you want useful prior samples.

Do not expect the ELBO to equal the exact likelihood in general.
