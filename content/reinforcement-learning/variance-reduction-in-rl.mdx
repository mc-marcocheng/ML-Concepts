<TLDR>
RL gradients often have high variance because rewards are noisy and delayed. Baselines, advantage estimates, bootstrapping, clipping, and normalization reduce variance.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

Policy-gradient estimates can vary greatly across trajectories.

High variance slows learning and can destabilize updates.

Variance reduction tries to make estimates less noisy.

Some methods keep the estimator unbiased.

Other methods add bias for lower variance.

## Baselines

A baseline subtracts a value that does not depend on the sampled action.

The common choice is $V(s)$.

This gives the advantage:

$$
A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s).
$$

The expected policy gradient stays the same.

The variance often becomes smaller.

## Reward and advantage normalization

Many implementations normalize advantages in a batch:

$$
\hat A
=
\frac{A-\operatorname{mean}(A)}
{\operatorname{std}(A)+\epsilon}.
$$

This changes scale and can improve optimization.

It does not solve biased value estimates.

## Bootstrapping and GAE

TD targets use bootstrapping to reduce return variance.

GAE mixes TD errors across multiple horizons.

The GAE parameter $\lambda$ controls the bias-variance tradeoff.

See <a href="/learn/reinforcement-learning/gae">GAE</a>.

## Importance weight control

Off-policy corrections can have high variance.

Clipping importance weights reduces variance.

It also adds bias.

PPO clips policy ratios for a related reason.

See <a href="/learn/reinforcement-learning/ppo">PPO</a>.

## Common mistakes

Do not assume lower variance always improves final performance.

Do not normalize rewards across tasks without checking meaning.

Do not use clipped weights without tracking the bias.
