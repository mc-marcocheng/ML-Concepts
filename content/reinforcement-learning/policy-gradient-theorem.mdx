<TLDR>
The policy gradient theorem turns return maximization into a gradient estimator. It uses the score function $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ times a value signal.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

Let $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ be a differentiable policy.

The objective is expected return:

$$
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[G_0].
$$

A common policy gradient form is:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}
\left[
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)
Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t,a_t)
\right].
$$

The score term changes the policy probability.

The value term says whether the action was good.

## REINFORCE estimator

A Monte Carlo estimator uses returns:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
\approx
\sum_t
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)G_t.
$$

This estimator is unbiased under standard assumptions.

It can have high variance.

Baselines reduce variance.

See <a href="/learn/reinforcement-learning/variance-reduction-in-rl">Variance Reduction in RL</a>.

## Baseline

You can subtract a state-only baseline:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}
\left[
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)
(Q(s_t,a_t)-b(s_t))
\right].
$$

The baseline does not change the expected gradient.

A common baseline is $V(s_t)$.

Then the weight becomes the advantage:

$$
A(s_t,a_t)=Q(s_t,a_t)-V(s_t).
$$

## Proof

<Proof title="Score function derivation">
Write the objective over trajectories:

$$
J(\theta)
=
\int p_\theta(\tau)R(\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

Differentiate under the integral:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\int \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)R(\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

Use the identity:

$$
\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)
=
p_\theta(\tau)\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau).
$$

Then:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau}
[
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)R(\tau)
].
$$

The trajectory log probability contains policy terms and dynamics terms.

Dynamics do not depend on $\theta$.

Thus:

$$
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)
=
\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t).
$$

This gives the REINFORCE estimator.
</Proof>

## Common mistakes

Do not differentiate through sampled actions directly for discrete actions.

Do not forget that baselines must not depend on the sampled action.

Do not ignore variance in long episodes.
