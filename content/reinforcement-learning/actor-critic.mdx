<TLDR>
Actor-critic methods use an actor to choose actions and a critic to estimate value. The critic supplies lower-variance learning signals for the actor.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

The actor is a policy:

$$
\pi_\theta(a \mid s).
$$

The critic estimates a value function.

Common critics estimate $V^\pi(s)$ or $Q^\pi(s,a)$.

The actor update uses a policy-gradient term:

$$
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\hat A_t.
$$

The critic helps compute $\hat A_t$.

## Critic training

A value critic can minimize squared TD error:

$$
L_V
=
\left(
V_\phi(s_t)-
[r_t+\gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})]
\right)^2.
$$

The critic does not need to be perfect.

It should give useful advantage estimates.

## Why use a critic

Pure Monte Carlo policy gradients can have high variance.

A critic reduces variance through bootstrapping and baselines.

This can add bias if the critic is inaccurate.

See <a href="/learn/reinforcement-learning/variance-reduction-in-rl">Variance Reduction in RL</a>.

## Common variants

A2C uses synchronous actor-critic updates.

A3C uses asynchronous workers.

PPO commonly uses an actor-critic architecture.

SAC uses actor-critic with entropy regularization.

See <a href="/learn/reinforcement-learning/soft-actor-critic">Soft Actor-Critic</a>.

## Common mistakes

Do not train the actor through the critic target by accident.

Do not ignore critic loss scale.

Do not assume a lower critic loss always gives a better policy.
