<TLDR>
Importance sampling reweights samples from a behavior distribution to estimate an expectation under a target distribution. It enables off-policy evaluation but can add high variance.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

Suppose samples come from $q(x)$.

You want an expectation under $p(x)$:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(x)].
$$

Rewrite it as:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(x)]
=
\mathbb{E}_{q}
\left[
\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}f(x)
\right].
$$

The ratio $p(x)/q(x)$ is the importance weight.

## Policy ratios

In reinforcement learning, a trajectory has probability under a policy.

For a trajectory $\tau$:

$$
w(\tau)
=
\frac{p_{\pi}(\tau)}{p_{\mu}(\tau)}.
$$

If the dynamics match, the ratio reduces to policy probabilities:

$$
w(\tau)
=
\prod_t
\frac{\pi(a_t \mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid s_t)}.
$$

Here, $\mu$ is the behavior policy.

The target policy is $\pi$.

## Variance problem

Products of ratios can become very large or very small.

This can cause high variance.

Common fixes include clipping, per-decision weights, and normalized weights.

PPO also uses policy ratios with clipping.

See <a href="/learn/reinforcement-learning/ppo">PPO</a>.

## Proof

<Proof title="Importance sampling identity">
Start with the target expectation:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(x)]
=
\int f(x)p(x)\,dx.
$$

Multiply and divide by $q(x)$.

This requires $q(x)>0$ wherever $p(x)f(x)$ can be nonzero.

$$
\int f(x)p(x)\,dx
=
\int f(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,dx.
$$

This is an expectation under $q$:

$$
=
\mathbb{E}_{q}
\left[
\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}f(x)
\right].
$$
</Proof>

## Code example

```python
import numpy as np

target_prob = np.array([0.8, 0.2])
behavior_prob = np.array([0.5, 0.5])
actions = np.array([0, 1, 0])
rewards = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 1.0])

weights = target_prob[actions] / behavior_prob[actions]
estimate = np.mean(weights * rewards)
```

## Common mistakes

Do not use importance sampling when behavior probability is zero.

Do not ignore the variance of long products.

Do not clip weights without understanding the bias.
