<TLDR>
RoPE applies position-dependent rotations to queries and keys. The resulting dot product depends on relative position.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

RoPE means rotary positional embedding.

It does not add a position vector to token embeddings.

Instead, it rotates query and key vectors by a position-dependent angle.

Attention then uses the rotated vectors.

This makes relative positions affect dot products.

## Two-dimensional rotation

For a pair of features $(x_1,x_2)$ at position $m$, RoPE applies:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1' \\
x_2'
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos(m\theta) & -\sin(m\theta) \\
\sin(m\theta) & \cos(m\theta)
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1 \\
x_2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The model applies this to pairs of channels with different frequencies.

## Relative position property

Let $R_m$ be the rotation at position $m$.

For query $q$ at position $m$ and key $k$ at position $n$:

$$
(R_m q)^\top(R_n k)
=
q^\top R_{n-m}k.
$$

Thus the score depends on the relative offset $n-m$.

<Proof title="Relative position property of rotations">
Rotation matrices satisfy:

$$
R_m^\top R_n = R_{n-m}.
$$

The attention dot product is:

$$
(R_m q)^\top(R_n k).
$$

Move the first rotation to the middle:

$$
q^\top R_m^\top R_n k.
$$

Substitute the rotation identity:

$$
q^\top R_{n-m} k.
$$

Thus the score depends on the relative offset.
</Proof>

## Context extension

Many LLMs use RoPE.

Longer context can need RoPE scaling or interpolation.

These methods change the mapping from position to angle.

They try to reduce mismatch between training and inference lengths.

## Code example

```python
import torch

def apply_rope_pair(x, cos, sin):
    x1 = x[..., 0::2]
    x2 = x[..., 1::2]
    y1 = x1 * cos - x2 * sin
    y2 = x1 * sin + x2 * cos
    return torch.stack((y1, y2), dim=-1).flatten(-2)
```

## Common mistakes

Do not add RoPE to values.

Most implementations rotate queries and keys.

Do not confuse RoPE with learned absolute positions.

Do not assume context extension works without validation.
