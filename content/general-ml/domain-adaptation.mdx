<TLDR>
Domain adaptation targets the case where the source and target distributions differ but the task stays the same. Name the shift type first, because covariate shift, label shift, and concept shift need different fixes. Importance weighting, feature alignment, and self-training are the main tools.
</TLDR>

## Types of shift

Write the joint as $p(x,y)$. The factorization you choose names the shift.

| Shift | What changes | What stays fixed | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Covariate shift | $p(x)$ | $p(y \mid x)$ | New camera, same object labels |
| Label shift | $p(y)$ | $p(x \mid y)$ | Disease prevalence rises |
| Concept shift | $p(y \mid x)$ | $p(x)$ | Spam wording changes meaning |
| Domain shift | Both | Neither | Synthetic images to real images |

Diagnose before you fix. A method for covariate shift does nothing for concept shift.

## Covariate shift and importance weighting

Under covariate shift, $p_T(y\mid x) = p_S(y\mid x)$ but $p_T(x) \neq p_S(x)$.

<Proof title="Importance weighting corrects the risk under covariate shift">
The target risk is

$$
R_T(h)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_T}\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_T(y\mid x)}\big[\ell(h(x),y)\big].
$$

Under covariate shift the conditional is shared, so replace $p_T(y\mid x)$ by $p_S(y\mid x)$:

$$
R_T(h)
=
\int p_T(x)\int p_S(y\mid x)\,\ell(h(x),y)\,dy\,dx .
$$

Multiply and divide by $p_S(x)$ wherever $p_S(x) > 0$:

$$
=
\int p_S(x)\frac{p_T(x)}{p_S(x)}\int p_S(y\mid x)\,\ell(h(x),y)\,dy\,dx .
$$

Recognize the outer integral as an expectation under the source joint:

$$
R_T(h)
=
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p_S}\left[\frac{p_T(x)}{p_S(x)}\,\ell(h(x),y)\right].
$$

So the weighted source risk with weights $w(x) = p_T(x)/p_S(x)$ is an unbiased estimate of the target risk.
</Proof>

Two conditions matter.

1. Support. The proof requires $p_S(x) > 0$ wherever $p_T(x) > 0$. Otherwise the weight is undefined.
2. Variance. Large weights inflate the variance of the estimate. The effective sample size falls to roughly

$$
n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}.
$$

Clip the weights to control this.

### Estimating the weights

Train a classifier to tell source from target inputs. If it outputs $d(x) = \Pr[\text{target}\mid x]$ with equal sample sizes, then

$$
\frac{p_T(x)}{p_S(x)} = \frac{d(x)}{1-d(x)}.
$$

This is the density ratio trick. The same classifier also measures the shift. An AUC near 0.5 means the domains are hard to distinguish, so the shift is small.

## Label shift

Under label shift $p(x\mid y)$ is fixed and only the class prior changes.

Black box shift estimation recovers the new prior from the confusion matrix. Let $C$ be the source confusion matrix with $C_{jk} = \Pr_S[\hat{y}=j, y=k]$, and let $\mu_j = \Pr_T[\hat{y}=j]$ be the target prediction rates. Then solve

$$
C\, w = \mu ,
\qquad w_k = \frac{p_T(y=k)}{p_S(y=k)} .
$$

You then reweight the training loss, or adjust the predicted logits by $\log w_k$. This needs only unlabeled target data.

## Feature alignment

The idea is to learn a representation where source and target look the same, while it stays predictive of the label.

### Domain adversarial training

DANN trains three parts.

1. A feature extractor $f$.
2. A label predictor $c$ on top of $f$.
3. A domain discriminator $d$ on top of $f$.

The objective is

$$
\min_{f,c}\max_{d}\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(c\circ f) - \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{domain}}(d\circ f).
$$

A gradient reversal layer implements this. It is the identity in the forward pass and multiplies the gradient by $-\lambda$ in the backward pass.

### CORAL

Align the second order statistics of the two feature sets:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CORAL}}
=
\frac{1}{4d^2}\big\|\Sigma_S - \Sigma_T\big\|_F^2 .
$$

This is much simpler than adversarial training and often works nearly as well.

### MMD

Maximum mean discrepancy compares kernel mean embeddings:

$$
\text{MMD}^2
=
\left\|\mathbb{E}_{p_S}[\phi(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{p_T}[\phi(x)]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2 .
$$

## Self-training and test-time adaptation

Self-training predicts pseudo-labels on target data, keeps the confident ones, and retrains. Confirmation bias is the main risk, because early errors reinforce themselves.

Test-time adaptation changes the model at inference with no labels.

- Update the batch normalization statistics using target batches. This is cheap and often effective.
- TENT minimizes the prediction entropy on target batches by updating only the normalization affine parameters.

## Theory

Ben-David gave a bound on the target error:

$$
\epsilon_T(h)
\le
\epsilon_S(h)
+ \tfrac{1}{2}d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{D}_S,\mathcal{D}_T)
+ \lambda^\star ,
$$

where $\lambda^\star$ is the error of the best joint hypothesis on both domains.

The bound explains the strategy. Reduce the source error, reduce the divergence between the representations, and hope the shared optimal error is small. It also warns that if $\lambda^\star$ is large, no alignment method can succeed.

## Detection and monitoring

1. Train a domain classifier on inputs. A high AUC signals input shift.
2. Track the distribution of the predicted scores over time.
3. Track feature statistics per column with a test such as Kolmogorov-Smirnov or population stability index.
4. Track label distribution where labels arrive later.
5. Alarm on calibration drift, not only on accuracy.

## Code example

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

def importance_weights(X_source, X_target, clip=10.0):
    """Density ratio via a source versus target classifier."""
    X = np.vstack([X_source, X_target])
    y = np.concatenate([np.zeros(len(X_source)), np.ones(len(X_target))])
    clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(X, y)
    d = clf.predict_proba(X_source)[:, 1]
    w = d / np.clip(1.0 - d, 1e-6, None)
    return np.clip(w, 0.0, clip)

def effective_sample_size(w):
    return w.sum() ** 2 / np.sum(w ** 2)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Xs = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(1000, 3))
Xt = rng.normal(1.0, 1.0, size=(1000, 3))

w = importance_weights(Xs, Xt)
print(round(float(w.mean()), 3), round(float(effective_sample_size(w)), 1))
```

An effective sample size far below the raw count warns that the weighted estimate is noisy.

## Common mistakes

Do not apply covariate shift methods when the true problem is concept shift. Only new labels fix concept shift.

Do not use importance weights without clipping and without checking the effective sample size.

Do not align features so aggressively that class information disappears. Alignment must keep the label signal.

Do not trust a source validation score as an estimate of target performance.
