<TLDR>
Volume grows exponentially with dimension, so any fixed sample covers less of the space. Distances between random points also become almost equal, which weakens methods that rely on nearest neighbors.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

Let the data live in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Three effects appear as $d$ grows.

1. Sample sparsity. The number of points needed to cover the space grows exponentially in $d$.
2. Volume concentration. Almost all volume of a ball sits near its surface.
3. Distance concentration. The ratio of the farthest to the nearest distance goes to one.

## Sample sparsity

Split each axis of the unit cube into $m$ bins. The cube then has $m^d$ cells.

To keep one point per cell you need $n = m^d$ points.

For $m=10$ and $d=10$ you need $10^{10}$ points.

A local method such as <a href="/learn/general-ml/k-nearest-neighbours">k-nearest neighbours</a> needs neighbors that are truly close. In high dimensions such neighbors do not exist for a normal sample size.

## Volume near the surface

Take the unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Compare it with the inner ball of radius $1-\epsilon$.

The fraction of volume in the outer shell is

$$
1 - (1-\epsilon)^d .
$$

For $\epsilon = 0.01$ and $d = 500$ this fraction is about $0.993$.

<Proof title="Fraction of ball volume in the outer shell">
The volume of a ball of radius $r$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is

$$
V_d(r) = C_d r^d ,
$$

where $C_d$ depends only on the dimension.

The inner ball has volume

$$
V_d(1-\epsilon) = C_d (1-\epsilon)^d .
$$

The ratio of inner volume to total volume is

$$
\frac{C_d(1-\epsilon)^d}{C_d 1^d} = (1-\epsilon)^d .
$$

So the shell holds the remaining fraction

$$
1 - (1-\epsilon)^d .
$$

Because $0 < 1-\epsilon < 1$, the term $(1-\epsilon)^d$ goes to zero as $d$ grows. The shell fraction goes to one.
</Proof>

## Distance concentration

Draw $n$ independent points from a fixed distribution with independent coordinates. Let $D_{\min}$ and $D_{\max}$ be the smallest and largest distance from a query point.

Under mild conditions,

$$
\frac{D_{\max} - D_{\min}}{D_{\min}} \to 0
\quad \text{as } d \to \infty .
$$

The reason is simple. A squared Euclidean distance is a sum of $d$ per-axis terms. The mean of that sum grows like $d$. The standard deviation grows like $\sqrt{d}$. So the relative spread shrinks like $1/\sqrt{d}$.

Nearest neighbor search then loses meaning, because every point is about equally far away.

## Effect on models

High dimension raises variance for flexible models. See <a href="/learn/general-ml/bias-variance-tradeoff">bias-variance tradeoff</a>.

Distance based methods degrade first. Linear models with strong <a href="/learn/general-ml/regularisation-methods">regularisation</a> degrade last.

Kernel bandwidth selection also becomes hard, because a bandwidth that holds enough points is no longer local.

## Why real data still works

Real data often lies near a low dimensional manifold inside the high dimensional space. This is the manifold hypothesis.

The effective dimension is then much smaller than $d$.

Useful responses to high dimension include:

- feature selection and <a href="/learn/general-ml/dimensionality-reduction">dimensionality reduction</a>,
- structural priors such as convolution in <a href="/learn/general-ml/cnns">CNNs</a>,
- strong regularization,
- more data along the directions that matter.

## Code example

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

for d in [2, 10, 100, 1000]:
    x = rng.normal(size=(1000, d))
    q = rng.normal(size=(1, d))
    dist = np.linalg.norm(x - q, axis=1)
    ratio = (dist.max() - dist.min()) / dist.min()
    print(d, round(float(ratio), 3))
```

The printed ratio shrinks as the dimension grows.

## Common mistakes

Do not assume that adding features always helps. Extra noisy features raise variance.

Do not trust Euclidean distance in very high dimension without a check.

Do not confuse ambient dimension with intrinsic dimension.
