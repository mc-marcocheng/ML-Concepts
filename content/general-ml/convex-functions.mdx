<TLDR>
A convex function lies below the line segment between any two points. Any local minimum is also global.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

A function $f$ is convex if:

$$
f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)
\le
\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y),
$$

for all $x,y$ and $\lambda \in [0,1]$.

This means the graph bends upward.

## First-order condition

For differentiable convex $f$:

$$
f(y) \ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\top(y-x).
$$

The tangent plane is a global lower bound.

## Second-order condition

For twice differentiable $f$, convexity is equivalent to:

$$
\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0
$$

for all $x$ in the domain.

See <a href="/learn/linear-algebra/hessian">Hessian</a> and <a href="/learn/linear-algebra/positive-semi-definite">Positive Semi-Definite</a>.

## Local and global minima

Every local minimum of a convex function is global.

<Proof title="Local minimum is global for a convex function">
Let $x^\star$ be a local minimum.

Assume there is a point $y$ with:

$$
f(y) < f(x^\star).
$$

For $\lambda \in (0,1)$, define:

$$
z_\lambda = (1-\lambda)x^\star+\lambda y.
$$

Convexity gives:

$$
f(z_\lambda)
\le
(1-\lambda)f(x^\star)+\lambda f(y).
$$

Since $f(y)<f(x^\star)$:

$$
f(z_\lambda)<f(x^\star).
$$

For small $\lambda$, $z_\lambda$ is near $x^\star$.

This contradicts that $x^\star$ is a local minimum.

Therefore no such $y$ exists.
</Proof>

## Common mistakes

Do not assume every optimization problem is convex.

Do not confuse convex functions with convex sets.

Do not assume strict convexity when only convexity is given.
