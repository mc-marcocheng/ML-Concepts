<TLDR>
The Jacobian is the matrix of first partial derivatives. It gives the best local linear approximation to a vector function.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

For a function $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the Jacobian is:

$$
J_f(x)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

It has shape $m \times n$.

Rows correspond to output coordinates.

Columns correspond to input coordinates.

## Local linear approximation

Near a point $x$, a differentiable function satisfies:

$$
f(x+\Delta x)
\approx
f(x)+J_f(x)\Delta x.
$$

Thus, the Jacobian is the local linear map.

The <a href="/learn/linear-algebra/rank-span">Rank and Span</a> of the Jacobian describe local output directions.

## Chain rule

If $h(x)=g(f(x))$, then:

$$
J_h(x)=J_g(f(x))J_f(x).
$$

The order of multiplication matters.

<Proof title="Jacobian chain rule intuition">
Let $y=f(x)$.

A small input change gives:

$$
\Delta y \approx J_f(x)\Delta x.
$$

Then $g$ changes by:

$$
\Delta h \approx J_g(y)\Delta y.
$$

Substitute the first approximation:

$$
\Delta h \approx J_g(f(x))J_f(x)\Delta x.
$$

Thus:

$$
J_h(x)=J_g(f(x))J_f(x).
$$
</Proof>

## Scalar outputs

If $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the Jacobian is a row vector.

Many texts call its transpose the gradient.

## Code example

```python
import numpy as np

def f(x):
    return np.array([x[0] ** 2 + x[1], x[0] * x[1]])

x = np.array([2.0, 3.0])
J = np.array([[2 * x[0], 1.0],
              [x[1], x[0]]])

print(J)
```

## Common mistakes

Do not transpose the Jacobian shape by accident.

Do not multiply chain rule factors in the wrong order.

Do not confuse the Jacobian with the Hessian.
