<TLDR>
The Hessian is the matrix of second partial derivatives of a scalar function. It describes local curvature.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

For a scalar function $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the Hessian is:

$$
H_f(x)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_n} \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n \partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n^2}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

It is the Jacobian of the gradient.

See <a href="/learn/linear-algebra/jacobian">Jacobian</a>.

## Curvature

The Hessian controls the second-order Taylor approximation:

$$
f(x+\Delta x)
\approx
f(x)
+
\nabla f(x)^\top \Delta x
+
\frac{1}{2}\Delta x^\top H_f(x)\Delta x.
$$

The quadratic term gives curvature.

## Positive semi-definite Hessian

If the Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere, then the function is convex under standard smoothness conditions.

If the Hessian at a critical point is positive definite, the point is a strict local minimum.

See <a href="/learn/linear-algebra/positive-semi-definite">Positive Semi-Definite</a>.

## Eigenvalue view

Hessian eigenvectors give principal curvature directions.

Eigenvalues give curvature strength in those directions.

A negative eigenvalue means a descent direction exists at a critical point.

## Newton step

Newton's method uses curvature to update parameters:

$$
x_{\text{new}}
=
x - H_f(x)^{-1}\nabla f(x).
$$

In code, solve the linear system instead of forming $H^{-1}$.

## Code example

```python
import numpy as np

def hessian_quadratic(A):
    return A + A.T

A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0],
              [0.0, 3.0]])

H = hessian_quadratic(A)
print(H)
```

## Common mistakes

Do not use a Hessian for a vector-valued output without defining a scalar objective.

Do not confuse a PSD Hessian at one point with global convexity.

Do not compute a matrix inverse when a solver is enough.
