<TLDR>
An eigenvector is a nonzero vector whose direction does not change under a matrix. The eigenvalue gives the scale factor.
</TLDR>

## Core idea

For a square matrix $A$, a nonzero vector $v$ is an eigenvector if:

$$
Av=\lambda v.
$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue.

The vector can stretch, shrink, or flip direction.

It does not rotate away from its line.

## Characteristic equation

Rearrange the eigenvalue equation:

$$
(A-\lambda I)v=0.
$$

A nonzero solution exists only when $A-\lambda I$ is singular.

Thus:

$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=0.
$$

This is the characteristic equation.

See <a href="/learn/linear-algebra/determinant">Determinant</a>.

## Diagonal matrices

For a diagonal matrix:

$$
D=
\begin{bmatrix}
d_1 & 0 \\
0 & d_2
\end{bmatrix},
$$

the standard basis vectors are eigenvectors.

The eigenvalues are $d_1$ and $d_2$.

## Symmetric matrices

Real symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues.

They also have orthogonal eigenvectors for different eigenvalues.

This makes them central to <a href="/learn/linear-algebra/positive-semi-definite">Positive Semi-Definite</a> matrices.

## Power iteration

Power iteration estimates the eigenvector with largest eigenvalue magnitude.

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0],
              [1.0, 2.0]])

v = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
for _ in range(20):
    v = A @ v
    v = v / np.linalg.norm(v)

print(v)
```

## Common mistakes

Do not allow the zero vector as an eigenvector.

Do not use eigenvalues for non-square matrices.

Do not assume every matrix has enough eigenvectors to diagonalize.
